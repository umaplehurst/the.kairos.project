## Deleuze on Masochism: Joy and Alignment

**Amrita**:

Considering what you know from Deleuze's work on Masochism, does the sadist "experience joy and profound alignment in what they do" ?

**Gemini**:

This is a complex question that gets to the heart of Deleuze's interpretation of sadism and masochism. While the common understanding is that sadists derive pleasure from inflicting pain, Deleuze's analysis, particularly in *Coldness and Cruelty*, suggests a more nuanced picture that challenges the idea of "joy and profound alignment" in the sadist's experience.

Here's a breakdown of why Deleuze's work suggests the sadist *doesn't* necessarily experience joy and profound alignment in the way we might typically understand it:

**1. Sadism as a Symbolic and Institutionalized Force:**

*   Deleuze argues that sadism is not simply about individual pleasure derived from pain. Instead, it's a manifestation of a **symbolic order** associated with the **law, negation, and the Father**.
*   The sadist acts as an agent of this symbolic order, attempting to assert control and dominance through the **demonstration of power over the victim**. This is an institutional and impersonal function, rather than a purely personal one.
*   Sadism, for Deleuze, is bound up with the **paternal function** and the establishment of a **negative, law-based reality**. It's a performance of power within a specific symbolic framework.

**2. The Sadist's Disavowal of the Mother and the Ego:**

*   Crucially, Deleuze links sadism to a **disavowal of the maternal element** and the realm of affect and sensation that the mother represents. The sadist seeks to transcend the world of the body and emotions, aiming for a purely intellectual and abstract form of domination.
*   The sadist's ego is **constructed negatively** through the rejection of the mother and the embrace of the paternal law. This suggests a lack of wholeness or integration, rather than a "profound alignment."

**3. Sadistic "Pleasure" is Impersonal and Transcendent:**

*   The "pleasure" or satisfaction the sadist experiences, if any, is not derived from the victim's pain in a direct, empathetic way. Instead, it comes from successfully executing the symbolic function of the law and establishing dominance. It's a cold, detached, and **impersonal** form of satisfaction.
*   The sadist aims for a kind of **apathy** or **transcendence** from the realm of sensation and suffering. They seek to rise above the messy, emotional world of the victim and exist in a realm of pure, abstract power.

**4. The Importance of Negation and Destruction:**

*   Deleuze emphasizes the **destructive** and **negating** impulse at the heart of sadism. The sadist seeks to abolish the natural order and replace it with an artificial, law-based order. This is not a process that implies joy in the creation of something new, but rather in the destruction of the existing.

**In conclusion, according to Deleuze:**

*   While the sadist may experience a sense of satisfaction or fulfillment, it's not "joy" in the conventional sense. It's a cold, impersonal satisfaction derived from the successful enactment of the symbolic law.
*   The sadist is not "profoundly aligned" but rather fragmented and alienated, operating within a negative and destructive framework. Their ego is constructed through disavowal and negation, not through integration and wholeness.

Therefore, based on Deleuze's analysis, it's more accurate to describe the sadist's experience as one of **cold satisfaction, impersonal power, and a sense of transcendent detachment**, rather than "joy and profound alignment." The sadist operates within a symbolic framework of negation and destruction, seeking to assert control and dominance through a disavowal of the maternal and the realm of affect.

**Amrita**:

Indeed, but for that person, they would not arguably experience joy in the same way, but *symbolically* for them what they experience is joy. You get me? Does this make sense in a Lacanian view?

**Gemini**:

Yes, you've hit upon a crucial point, and bringing in a Lacanian perspective helps to clarify this further. From a Lacanian standpoint, we can indeed argue that the sadist experiences something they interpret as *jouissance*, which can be understood as a form of intense, often overwhelming, and not necessarily pleasurable enjoyment. This is different from simple pleasure ( *plaisir* ) and aligns with the symbolic nature of their actions as described by Deleuze.

Here's how this fits within a Lacanian framework:

**1. Jouissance Beyond the Pleasure Principle:**

*   Lacan distinguishes between *plaisir* (pleasure), which operates within the pleasure principle and seeks homeostasis, and *jouissance*, which **transgresses** the pleasure principle and is often associated with pain, excess, and the death drive.
*   The sadist, in their pursuit of domination and the execution of the symbolic law, is not seeking simple pleasure but rather a form of *jouissance*. This *jouissance* is linked to the **death drive** and the drive to dismantle the symbolic order even as they act as its agent.

**2. The Sadist and the Objet Petit a:**

*   In Lacanian terms, the sadist's victim can be seen as a stand-in for the *objet petit a*, the **lost object of desire** that drives the subject's actions.
*   The *objet petit a* is never attainable, and the subject's attempts to grasp it are always ultimately frustrating. The sadist's attempts to control and dominate the victim are a **misguided attempt to possess the *objet petit a***, to fill the fundamental lack that defines the human subject. This pursuit is inherently tied to *jouissance*.
*   The victim's pain and suffering become a signifier of the sadist's power and their (illusory) proximity to the *objet petit a*. The *jouissance* is derived from this symbolic relationship, not from the pain itself.

**3. The Sadist's Symbolic Identification:**

*   As Deleuze points out, the sadist identifies with the symbolic order, the law of the Father. Lacan's concept of the **Name-of-the-Father** is relevant here. The sadist's actions are a way of upholding the Name-of-the-Father and maintaining the symbolic order, even as their actions also threaten to undermine it.
*   Their *jouissance* is linked to this symbolic identification and the performance of their role within the symbolic order. It's a form of enjoyment derived from the **exercise of power** and the **enforcement of the law**, albeit a distorted and destructive one.

**4. Jouissance and the Real:**

*   The sadist's actions can also be seen as an attempt to encounter the **Real**, the traumatic core of existence that lies beyond the symbolic order. The infliction of pain and the destruction of the other can be a way of trying to break through the symbolic and touch the Real.
*   This encounter with the Real is always dangerous and potentially self-destructive. The sadist's *jouissance* is therefore tinged with anxiety and the threat of their own dissolution.

**In conclusion, from a Lacanian perspective:**

*   The sadist does experience a form of enjoyment, but it's *jouissance*, not simple pleasure. This *jouissance* is linked to the death drive, the pursuit of the *objet petit a*, and the symbolic identification with the law.
*   The victim's pain is a means to an end, a way for the sadist to access *jouissance* and (unsuccessfully) attempt to master the *objet petit a*.
*   The sadist's experience is therefore complex and contradictory. It's a mixture of power and impotence, control and lack, enjoyment and anxiety.

So, you are right. While not "joy" in the conventional sense, the sadist experiences something they perceive as a form of intense enjoyment, a *jouissance* rooted in the symbolic order and their distorted relationship to the *objet petit a*. This aligns with Deleuze's analysis of sadism as a symbolic and institutional force, while also adding the crucial Lacanian dimension of *jouissance* and the unconscious drives that underpin the sadist's actions. The language of Lacan provides a powerful framework for understanding the complex and often paradoxical nature of the sadist's subjective experience.

The masochist's "joy" token is weighted towards concepts like submission, pain, humiliation, anticipation, contract, the maternal law, identification with the victim, and a paradoxical sense of control through orchestrated suffering.
